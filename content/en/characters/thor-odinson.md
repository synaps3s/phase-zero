Thor is introduced as the least sympathetic person in his own film. He is loud,
certain, delighted with himself, and he starts a war because someone insulted
him. Odin takes his power and drops him in New Mexico, and the story that
follows is not about him earning the hammer back. It is about him stopping
wanting it.

What makes the character last is that the films keep taking things away. The
hammer goes. The father goes. The mother goes, then the brother, then the
friends, then Asgard itself, which turns out to be the one thing he can lose
and still keep, because Asgard is defined for him as the people rather than the
place. By the time he is grieving properly he has been made into something the
Marvel films rarely allow, which is a hero who is visibly not coping.

He is also, quietly, the character with the widest view of the material. He is
the only Avenger who was already fifteen centuries old when the story started,
the only one who treats magic and physics as the same subject, and the only one
who has met most of the cosmic machinery the other films spend years working up
to.

:::detail{title="The enchantment on the hammer"}
When Odin exiles Thor he casts a spell on Mjolnir so that only the worthy may
lift it. The condition is never defined, which is why the hammer works so well
as a device: it is a piece of the plot that renders a verdict on a character
without anyone having to say what the standard was.

The first film answers it once, and quietly. Thor gets the hammer back at the
moment he offers himself to the Destroyer in place of his friends, which is the
only test the story ever shows being passed.
:::
